# Entry: 28 August 2026
Source material is the American Time Use Survey from the Bureau of Labor Statistics, the United Nations International Classification of Activities for Time-Use Statistics, and the academic literature on time poverty and discretionary time.

## 1. The finding that changes the pitch
The 2025 American Time Use Survey shows the average American aged 15 and over has about 5.2 hours a day of leisure and sports time. A separate analysis of the same dataset found no demographic subgroup averaging under 4.5 hours of free time a day.

Of that leisure time, 2.6 hours a day goes to watching television. Half of it.

Lower income and lower education groups reported more free time than college educated higher income groups, not less, and spent more of it on screens.

This matters because the folder as first written implied the client has no time and we will find it. On the numbers, most people already have several hours a day. What they do not have is any idea where it goes, and it arrives in pieces they cannot use.

So the honest promise is not that we will find them time. It is that we will show them the time they already have, in usable pieces, so they can decide what it goes to. That is the stronger claim anyway, because it can be proved inside a month.

Source: [US Bureau of Labor Statistics, American Time Use Survey 2025 results](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/atus.htm)

## 2. The structure the field uses
Time use research splits a day into three parts, and that split is the basis for every serious measure in the field.

**Necessary time.** Sleep, eating, personal care and grooming. Not negotiable.

**Committed time.** Paid work, plus unpaid domestic labor and caregiving. Obligations that are real even though nobody schedules them for you. Within this the field separates contracted time, meaning paid work, from committed time, meaning household and care.

**Discretionary time.** What is left after the first two.

Discretionary time is the exact quantity this folder calls the hours a client controls. That number is not something we invented. It has a literature, thresholds, and decades of measurement behind it.

Clair Vickery, an American economist, proposed in 1977 that a person needs at least ten hours a week of discretionary time. Later researchers have used two hours a day, or a proportion of the population median, instead. There is no single agreed figure, which means we can pick a defensible one and say why.

Source: [Time poverty thresholds in the USA](https://link.springer.com/rwe/10.1007/978-94-007-0753-5_3704)

## 3. Two classification systems worth taking from
The Bureau of Labor Statistics is the United States federal agency that measures employment, prices, and how people spend their days. Since 2003 it has run the American Time Use Survey, which codes activities into 17 major lexicon categories, published as roughly a dozen: personal care, eating and drinking, household activities, purchasing goods and services, caring for household members, caring for non household members, working and work related, education, organizational and civic and religious, leisure and sports, telephone and mail and email, and a residual.

The United Nations Statistics Division maintains the International Classification of Activities for Time-Use Statistics, adopted in 2017. It uses nine major divisions: employment, production of goods for own use, unpaid domestic services, unpaid caregiving, unpaid volunteer and trainee work, learning, socializing and community and religious practice, culture and leisure and media and sports, and self care and maintenance.

Its stated design principles are worth copying directly. Categories must be mutually exclusive and exhaustive, stable, well described, and well balanced, meaning not too many and not too few.

**The most useful detail.** The American survey codes travel according to the purpose of the travel. Driving to work is coded as travel related to working, not as a separate transport category. That is structurally identical to the cost of field in this folder. An international standard arrived at our design independently, which is good evidence it is correct.

Source: [ATUS activity coding lexicon](https://www.bls.gov/tus/lexicons/lexiconwex2023.pdf)

Four to eight is time committed to something or someone. Nine to eleven is time the client chose. Twelve measures how much of the week the client controls.

Sleep. Getting ready. Travel. Paid work. Unpaid work time. Household time. Caring for people. Appointments. Time with people. Rest and leisure. Time on what you are building. Control over your week.

## 5. What the scale measures
Resolved 28 August 2026. The scale measures how well accounted for a kind of time is. It does not measure how well the client lives. Ten means the client knows where those hours go and it is how they want it. One means they do not know.

A client who sleeps five hours a night on purpose, knows it, and wants it that way is a ten. The scale does not judge the choice.

A low start is a fact about the record, not a verdict on the client. Most clients will start low across most questions, because most people do not account for their time.

## 6. Research behind the teaching layer
Seneca was a Roman statesman and philosopher who advised the emperor Nero and died in 65 AD. In an essay written around 49 AD, De Brevitate Vitae, meaning On the Shortness of Life, he argued that life is not short but that people waste it. He observed that people guard property fiercely and go to arms over a boundary dispute, while allowing others to take possession of their time without objection.

Source: [Seneca, On the Shortness of Life, full text](https://archive.org/stream/SenecaOnTheShortnessOfLife/Seneca+on+the+Shortness+of+Life_djvu.txt)

Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky were psychologists whose work on how people misjudge risk and probability changed economics. Kahneman received the Nobel Prize in Economics in 2002. In 1979 they named the planning fallacy: people underestimate how long a task will take even when they have done it many times before. Three psychologists, Roger Buehler, Dale Griffin and Michael Ross, tested it in 1994 by asking students to predict when they would finish their theses. The average prediction was under thirty four days and most took considerably longer.

Source: [Buehler, Griffin and Ross, Exploring the planning fallacy, 1994](https://web.mit.edu/curhan/www/docs/Articles/biases/67_J_Personality_and_Social_Psychology_366,_1994.pdf)

Sophie Leroy is an organizational psychologist at the University of Washington. In 2009 she established what she called attention residue. When a person switches tasks, part of their attention stays on the first one and their performance on the second measurably suffers. Gloria Mark, a professor of informatics at the University of California, Irvine, found that after an interruption a worker takes around twenty five minutes to return to what they were doing. This is the evidence behind treating the longest unbroken block as more useful than the total.

Source: [Sophie Leroy on attention residue, University of Washington](https://www.uwb.edu/news/2024/05/17/an-ongoing-study-on-the-success-of-staying-focused)

Source material is the published reviews of time management, planning, and progress monitoring, the instruments occupational therapists use to assess time management, the World Health Organization classification of functioning, and the Bureau of Labor Statistics release that the August 28 entry cited.

## 1. The finding that changes the folder
Time management has been measured across 158 studies and 53,957 people. It is related to wellbeing more strongly than to performance. The link with wellbeing is r \= .313. The link with job performance is r \= .259. The link with scores on standardized tests is r \= .011.

T. H. Macan studied 353 employees in 1994. Time management behaviors were related to lower tension and higher job satisfaction. They were not related to job performance as rated by supervisors.

This matters because the folder cannot promise that a client will get more done. The evidence supports a smaller claim. A client who plans and reviews carries less strain. That fits Version 4, which does not measure how much a client does.

Most of these studies describe students and employed adults at one point in time. The check found no study of adults without stable housing and no study of adults on shift work. The search for one was not exhaustive.

Source: [Aeon, Faber and Panaccio, Does time management work? A meta-analysis, 2021](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0245066)

Source: [Macan, Time management: test of a process model, 1994](https://doi.org/10.1037/0021-9010.79.3.381)

## 2. The structure the field uses
Researchers describe carrying out an intention as a cycle with four parts. A person decides what to commit to, plans it, acts on it, and reviews what happened. The review is used in the next plan.

Brad Aeon and Herman Aguinis define time management as “a form of decision making used by individuals to structure, protect, and adapt their time to changing conditions.”

**Structure.** Planning what will happen and when.

**Protect.** Deciding what to take on and what to decline.

**Adapt.** Changing the plan when conditions change.

The parts are connected. Time management is related to conscientiousness at r \= .451, so the parts share one underlying disposition. An occupational therapy instrument with 30 items was tested in 2022. Its items formed three groups: time management, organization and planning, and regulation of emotion. Neither result supports twelve separate abilities.

Source: [Roshanay and others, Occupational Therapy International, 2022](https://doi.org/10.1155/2022/6949102)

## 3. What helps a person carry out a plan
Three practices have the strongest support.

**A plan that names when, where, and how.** Across 642 tests, a plan in the form “if this happens, I will do that” improved outcomes. The effects ran from d \= .27 to d \= .66. The effect was larger when the person wanted the outcome and rehearsed the plan.

**A review that is put on file.** Across 138 experiments with 19,951 people, a prompt to check progress improved results, d \= 0.40. The effect was larger when the progress was written down or reported to another person. This is the evidence behind Daily Close.

**A reminder.** In a Cochrane review, attendance at health appointments was 67.8 percent with no reminder and 78.6 percent with a text reminder. In a review of 16 trials with 2,742 patients, text messages raised the odds of taking medication as prescribed, with an odds ratio of 2.11. Both reviews cover health care.

A recurring action takes longer than a month to become automatic. In a study of 96 volunteers, the time ran from 18 to 254 days. Missing one day did not change the result.

Source: [Sheeran, Listrom and Gollwitzer, The when and how of planning, 2025](https://doi.org/10.1080/10463283.2024.2334563)

Source: [Harkin and others, Does monitoring goal progress promote goal attainment?, 2016](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000025)

Source: [Cochrane review, Mobile phone messaging reminders for attendance at healthcare appointments, 2013](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD007458.pub3)

Source: [Thakkar and others, Mobile telephone text messaging for medication adherence in chronic disease, 2016](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamainternmed.2015.7667)

Source: [Lally and others, How are habits formed, 2010](https://doi.org/10.1002/ejsp.674)

## 4. Support and what a client does alone
The World Health Organization classification of functioning keeps two measures apart.

**Capacity.** What a person does with no help.

**Performance.** What a person does in their real setting, including the devices and the people who help them.

The classification files both. It does not lower performance because help was used.

This is the model for Time. What a client does with the schedule and the agent is one fact. What the client does alone is a second fact. The amount of support is a third.

Two older rating scales take the opposite position. Each lowers a score when a person has help from another person. Treating support as neutral is a program position.

Knowing when to rely on a reminder is itself a skill. In laboratory studies people set more reminders than they needed. Lower confidence in their own memory predicted it.

One trial tested time management training for adults with attention deficit hyperactivity disorder, autism, or a mental disorder. It randomized 88 people, and 75 gave data at the start. A group program and individual occupational therapy both improved time management skills. Neither format did better than the other on the main outcome.

Source: [World Health Organization, Towards a Common Language for Functioning, Disability and Health](https://cdn.who.int/media/docs/default-source/classification/icf/icfbeginnersguide.pdf)

Source: [Lidström Holmqvist and others, Effectiveness of a group-based time-management intervention, 2026](https://doi.org/10.1080/09638288.2026.2614218)

## 6. What the scale measures
Proposed October 11, 2026. The scale measures how well a client plans a commitment and carries it out under the conditions the client set. It does not measure how much a client does.

Four findings affect how the options are written.

**An option describes what the client does.** Four of the proposed top options describe the schedule, with wording such as “is on my schedule without effort.” A client with a working agent would select ten whatever the client does alone.

**“Without effort” cannot be observed.** The Language Standard requires a start option that another person could observe.

**“Every” and “each” set one standard for all clients.** Version 4 compares an outcome with the conditions that client set.

**A client cannot report accuracy before it is measured.** How close an estimate is, and how often a plan is carried out, need outcomes on file. A start selected at Enrollment is the client’s own report. The research supports selecting it again after a period of Plans and Daily Close outcomes. No study gives the length of that period.

Ten written options have not been tested. Whether a client can tell ten levels apart is unknown.

## 7. Corrections to the August 28 entry
The August 28 text stays as written. Each correction below is dated October 11, 2026.

**Leisure hours, section 1.** The figures are correct: 5.2 hours of leisure and sports a day, and 2.6 hours of television. Two limits apply. The 2025 survey has no data for September 30 through November 11, 2025. It interviewed about 6,100 people.

**No group under 4.5 hours, section 1.** The statement is from Sturm and Cohen and uses 2014 to 2016 data from 32,048 people. It describes group averages. It does not describe one person.

**No idea where it goes, section 1.** No source supports this sentence. The check found no direct evidence that free time reaches a client in pieces too short to use.

**Three parts of a day, section 2.** The scheme has four parts: necessary, contracted, committed, and free time.

**Ten hours a week, section 2.** Clair Vickery did not propose ten hours of discretionary time. A 2016 paper reports that her figure was 81.4 hours a week of necessary time, with necessary leisure included. The August 28 sentence is withdrawn.

**Picking a threshold, sections 2 and 7.** No study establishes a healthy number of discretionary hours. A threshold needs a program decision. The evidence does not supply one.

**Seventeen categories and travel, section 3.** Correct. The survey has 17 major categories and codes travel by its purpose. The sentence that calls this good evidence the design is correct is withdrawn. Two classification systems that agree do not show the design fits a different use.

**The planning fallacy, section 6.** The thesis figures are correct. Students predicted 33.9 days and took 55.5. The fallacy is established for long tasks with many steps. Reviews report that people overestimate short tasks. The statement that people underestimate a task they have done many times is too broad.

**Twenty five minutes, section 6.** The figure is misdescribed. Mark, Gonzalez and Harris observed 24 workers. Interrupted work was resumed after 25 minutes 26 seconds on average, and the worker did other work in between. That is the time before returning to the work. It is not the time needed to regain focus. A second figure in wide use, 23 minutes 15 seconds, comes from a 2006 Gallup interview with Gloria Mark.

**The longest unbroken block, section 6.** The check found no study that compares one unbroken block with the same total time in pieces. It stays as a program position.

**The prize, section 6.** The full name is the Sveriges Riksbank Prize in Economic Sciences in Memory of Alfred Nobel.

## 2. Corrections to the first October 11 entry
Each correction below is dated October 11, 2026.

**Daily Close, section 3.** Harkin and others supports progress monitoring. It does not test Daily Close. No study in it is a daily conversation with another person about the commitments of that day. The authors state that only one study concerned a subject unrelated to health. The effect falls from d \= 0.40 to d \= 0.19 after their adjustment for unpublished studies. A prompt to review what a person set out to do added no significant benefit. The sentence that calls this the evidence behind Daily Close is withdrawn. Daily Close is a design that follows from the finding. No study has tested it.

**What the finding does support, section 3.** Two parts of the finding are closest to Daily Close. The effect was larger when progress was reported to another person, d \= 0.47, than when it was kept private, d \= 0.19. The effect was larger when progress was written down, d \= 0.43, than when it was not, d \= 0.29.

**Capacity and performance, section 4.** The World Health Organization distinction is a framework for thinking about assistance. It is not a validated scoring instrument for Time. The guide gives a way to code what a person does with and without assistance. The World Health Organization built a separate instrument for assessment. No tested scale exists for what a client does alone and what a client does with the schedule and the agent.

**Two older rating scales, section 4.** The statement holds for the Functional Independence Measure. For the Lawton scale it holds in part. Help from another person lowers the score for shopping, medication, meals, and laundry. It does not lower the score for housekeeping, travel, or money.

**Reminders as a skill, section 4.** The task was a memory task of about one minute, on a tablet or a computer. One experiment had 41 adults in a laboratory. One had 268 adults in the United States online. The study did not observe the reminders a person uses in daily life.

**Time management training, section 4.** Both groups in the trial received training. No group went without. The trial shows that both groups improved. It does not show that the training caused the improvement.

**A cycle with four parts, section 2.** The 1987 paper does not set out four parts. A 1990 chapter by Gollwitzer names four phases: deciding, planning, acting, and evaluating. The idea that reflection on one attempt is used in the next plan comes from Zimmerman, 2000, who names three phases: forethought, performance, and self reflection. Neither was read. Both are known through later papers.

**Three groups of items, section 2.** The three groups were taken from an earlier Swedish study and then tested on this sample. They held for 27 of the 30 items.

**The planning fallacy, section 7.** Two sentences are withdrawn: that the fallacy is established for long tasks with many steps, and that reviews report people overestimate short tasks. Halkjelsvik and Jørgensen found underestimation more often in engineering and management studies. They did not find it in psychology studies. They question the finding that small tasks are overestimated and state that random error can produce it in part.

**Short tasks, section 8.** One review, by Roy, Christenfeld and McKenzie in 2005, reports that tasks under five minutes are more likely to be overestimated. It was read as an excerpt of the authors’ copy. The direction of the error is unsettled. The lesson holds when a client compares their own estimate with what happened.

## 3. Association and cause
The review of 158 studies in section 1 of the first October 11 entry reports correlations. Most of those studies measured people at one point in time. A correlation shows that two measures move together. It does not show that one causes the other.

One sentence in that section is corrected. It said a client who plans and reviews carries less strain. The evidence supports this much: people who report more time management also report higher wellbeing. The studies do not show which one comes first.

Cause is shown in three places. The 138 studies of progress monitoring were randomized. The appointment reminder studies were randomized trials. The medication reminder studies were randomized trials.

## 4. What supports the practice and what is analogy
Each finding gives Time one of four kinds of support.

**Supports the practice.** A study tested the same practice in another setting.

**Analogy.** A study tested a different task or a different group of people. The finding is applied to Time by inference.

**Framework.** A way to organize the subject. No test stands behind its use in Time.

**Association.** Two measures move together. No cause is shown.
